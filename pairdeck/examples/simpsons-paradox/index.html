Simpson's paradox

How can a treatment win in every group and still lose overall?

A PairDeck example · data from a 1986 study of kidney-stone treatments

700 patients, two kinds of stones, two treatments

Each patient had small or large stones, and got treatment A or B.

Data: 700 patients in a 1986 comparison of kidney-stone treatments (Charig and colleagues, BMJ).

Small stones or large, A succeeds more often

In both groups A does better: by 6 points on small stones, by 4 on large ones.

Small stones: A 81 of 87, B 234 of 270. Large stones: A 192 of 263, B 55 of 80.

Put the groups together, and B wins

The same patients and the same outcomes, yet the totals point the other way.

A: 273 of 350 succeeded. B: 289 of 350.

A got most of the hard cases

Large stones are harder for both, and A treated three times as many.

Each total is an average of the two group rates, weighted by this mix: A's total leans on its 73%, B's on its 87%.

Move A's mix and watch its total

75%

A's group rates never change; only the mix does. Here B looks better.

Computed from the study's group rates: A's total = 93.1% × (1 − share) + 73.0% × share. It drops below B's 83% once more than about 52% of A's patients have large stones.

Give both the same patients, and A comes out ahead

On the same mix of patients A is ahead by 5 points, as it is within each group.

Computed: each treatment's group rates weighted by the mix of all 700 patients (357 small, 343 large stones). This assumes stone size is what drove both the choice of treatment and the outcome.

Read a total with its mix in mind

  1. 1Totals mix two thingsHow well a treatment works, and who received it.
  2. 2Split by what drove the choiceWhen a factor shapes both who gets what and how it turns out, compare within its groups.
  3. 3Not every split is the right oneDo not split by something the treatment itself changes; the causal story decides.
